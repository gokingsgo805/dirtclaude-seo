Check a BLM mining claim before you drive

dirtClaude shows active Bureau of Land Management mining claims on a bare-earth LiDAR and roads basemap, so a prospector can see who holds the ground before a field day starts. The map pairs claim locations with historical mines, adits, and mineral occurrences.

Updated 12 October 2026. The General Mining Law of 1872 still frames how locatable-mineral claims are staked on federal public land. This is a field check, not a legal title opinion.

What you see on the claim

Open a claim and dirtClaude draws it against the bare-earth surface and the roads that actually reach it. Recorded claimant details come from the BLM claim layer. Use that to decide whether the ground is open, claimed, or worth a closer look at the official serial number.

  • Claim location on the LiDAR hillshade, not a photo basemap that hides the ground.
  • Roads and tracks into the site.
  • Nearby historical mines, adits, and mineral occurrences.

What a claim does not allow

An active mining claim is a right to locatable minerals, not a fence around the desert. Collecting material, driving off route, or camping on a claim can still be illegal. Confirm the serial number with BLM MLRS records before you work the ground.

How to check before you leave

  1. Search the claim or meridian in dirtClaude.
  2. Read the claimant and the location against the roads layer.
  3. Match the serial number on the BLM minerals site.

The Bureau of Land Management publishes the rules for locatable minerals, including how claims are recorded and maintained.

What does dirtClaude show for a BLM claim?

dirtClaude shows the claim on a bare-earth LiDAR and roads basemap, plus recorded claimant details from the BLM layer and nearby mine and mineral records.

Does a mapped claim mean you can collect there?

No. Mapping a claim tells you the ground is recorded to someone else. It is not permission to dig, collect, or drive off designated routes.
